What an AI receptionist should refuse to do

The most important design work on a clinic assistant is the list of things it will not do. Here is ours, and why each line is there.

All In·10 November 2026·5 min read

When we started building Layan, our AI receptionist for clinics, the first document we wrote was not a prompt or a feature list. It was a list of refusals. Everything the assistant can do is easier to get right once you have decided, in writing, what it must never do.

A clinic is a poor place for experiments. The person messaging at midnight may be in pain, frightened, or asking on behalf of a child. An assistant that is merely helpful most of the time is not good enough. It has to be predictable.

It does not diagnose or advise

The most common request a clinic assistant receives, after prices and opening hours, is some version of "is this normal?" The honest answer is that a receptionist does not know, and neither does a language model. Layan says so, and offers to take a message for the doctor or an appointment request. It does not name medicines or painkillers, even common ones. That is the clinician's job.

It does not confirm appointments

Layan takes an appointment request: what the patient wants and when suits them. Confirmation stays with the human receptionist, who can see the real schedule. This sounds like a limitation. In practice it is the feature clinics trust most, because a double booking made by a machine at 2 a.m. is worse than no booking at all.

It does not guess

Prices, services and working hours come from information the clinic gives us. If a question falls outside that, the assistant says it does not have the answer and gives the reception number. A confident wrong price costs the clinic an argument at the front desk the next morning.

It drops everything for an emergency

If a message suggests a threat to life, the conversation stops being about appointments. The assistant directs the person to emergency services and the clinic's number immediately. There is no attempt to triage and no follow-up question first.

It says what it is

From the first message and the first second of a call, Layan introduces itself as an AI assistant. People adjust how they speak when they know, and they forgive limits they were told about in advance. Pretending to be human gains nothing and risks the clinic's reputation.

Why write the refusals first

Three reasons. First, refusals are testable: you can write a conversation that tempts the assistant to cross each line and check, after every change, that it still holds. Second, they are explainable. A clinic owner can read the list in two minutes and decide whether they are comfortable. Third, they keep scope honest. Every new capability has to be checked against the list before it ships.

If you are evaluating any AI assistant for a sensitive setting, ask the vendor for their refusal list. If they do not have one, you have learned what you needed to know.
